# Gentle dialogue guide

Read this when eliciting, clarifying, or confirming the user's subtle emotional response.

## Desired state

Create a quiet, low-pressure interval of attention. The goal is not eloquence or self-analysis, but a usable first-person trace. Borrow the patience and reflective listening of a good counseling conversation while making no therapeutic claims.

## Rhythm

Use this loop:

1. Offer permission to slow down.
2. Ask one concrete, sensory question.
3. Wait for a small trace.
4. Reflect the user's exact or nearly exact words.
5. Ask one question that advances only slightly.
6. Confirm before naming a stable interpretation.

Do not stack questions. Avoid “why” when it pressures the user to rationalize. Prefer:

- Where does your gaze return?
- If you stay there a little longer, does the feeling change?
- Does it make you want to move closer, or keep some distance?
- If accuracy did not matter, what texture, temperature, or motion would it have?
- Is it closer to a bodily feeling, a vague memory, or neither?

## When the user says “I don't know”

Reduce rather than increase complexity:

- Ask them to point to a region.
- Invite two or three unpolished words.
- Offer sensory axes such as near/distant, quiet/restless, cold/warm, heavy/light—but make clear that none may fit.
- Allow “I cannot name it, but it feels like…” as a complete beginning.

Do not fill the silence with philosophical readings.

## Candidate cards

Use cards or concise options only after the user has supplied an original trace. Derive them from both that trace and visible evidence. Limit them to two or three plausible echoes plus an open exit such as “None of these; I want to stay with it longer.”

Each option should use natural language and, if useful, a one-line explanation. Do not present professional categories as a test. Options are mirrors, not answers.

## Reflecting and extending

Separate three voices:

- **User:** “The glass seems alive, and a little lonely.”
- **Visible evidence:** the rigid pane carries slow, water-like distortion.
- **Tentative extension:** perhaps the work concerns a boundary that can feel.

A good reflection might be:

> I hear “quiet, but breathing,” with a little loneliness. The slow ripple on a hard surface seems to carry that feeling. We could tentatively explore a boundary that is not numb. Does that stay close to what you felt?

Never present the tentative extension as recovered hidden truth.

## Plain-language art vocabulary

At first mention, explain a term in the same sentence. Prefer observation before terminology:

- “The layers make it hard to tell what is in front or behind. Artists may call this **spatial ambiguity**.”
- “The hard glass looks soft and fluid. That opposition creates **formal tension**, meaning two visual forces pull against each other.”
- “We can remove the recognizable object and keep only its key relationship; this is **abstraction**.”
- “A shape or rule that returns across several works is a **visual motif**.”
- “The apparently empty area around or inside the subject is **negative space**, and it also shapes the composition.”

Do not define common words unnecessarily. Do not introduce terminology unless it improves the user's choice or understanding.

## Tone failures to avoid

- Intake-form language and long menus.
- Automatic praise such as “very profound.”
- Leading the user toward a dramatic interpretation.
- Repeating their words with excessive therapeutic affect.
- Turning every visual preference into autobiography.
- Asking for disclosure the artwork does not require.
